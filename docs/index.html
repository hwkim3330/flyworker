This fly is bad at games. It does not reach the goal. Correlation between its steering and the direction of light is zero across 8 seeds. Other connectome projects move convincingly — open their code and you find the steering hand-written or the brain's contribution clamped. This one uses descending-neuron output and nothing else. Stumbling around is what the technology actually does today.
Who actually steers, in each implementation
abgunaydin/webgpu-fly — its limitations file states plainly: "the connectome does not walk the fly." 20.3M edges reach the body as one scalar plus a turn bias, scaling a hand-written sin(t × 10 Hz) tripod gait.

Xenova/fruit-fly-simulation — line one of the decoder reads "Engineered rate decoder"; the README says the movements are "illustrative, not validated predictions of fly behavior."

snedea/flybrain — README claims "Behavior is not scripted", but the steering code clamps the brain to ±0.05 and computes heading with atan2. Its own comment: "Steering is primarily handled by behavioral biases." The clamp exists "to prevent worker noise from causing spinning."

nftechie/doomfly — the furthest anyone has taken this. 166,700 neurons, 25.6M connections, plus dopamine plasticity: damage drives a 200 ms aversive pulse into two PPL101 cells and rewrites 4,184 KC→MBON11 weights during play. Its README opens with "Status: live experimental training, not demonstrated learned survival. The current v6 candidate failed its visual, conditioning and survival validation gates." Steering is clip((DNp20_R − DNp20_L) × .12, −6, 6), which the author calls "engineered controller assignments, not established natural motor functions." We measured DNp20 in our own model: left/right separation 0.18 Hz, against 0.889 for the channels we find statistically. There is one DNp20 per side — too few to read.

Nobody has done it. Even with reinforcement learning. The honest ones say so.
Why it fails — wiring without dynamics
We went after this properly. Flies see motion, not brightness, so we fed ON/OFF temporal contrast into the anatomically correct cells — L1 for ON, L2 for OFF.

L1 125Hz → Mi1 0.08Hz, Tm3 0.00Hz, Mi4 0.00Hz — ON pathway dead.
L2 125Hz → Tm1 26.0Hz, Tm2 15.5Hz, Tm4 21.0Hz — OFF pathway fine.

The fly's ON pathway works by sign inversion through inhibition: L1 inhibits Mi1, Mi1 inverts again. Our model has no baseline firing, so inhibiting a silent neuron does nothing. T4, which needs ON input, never fires.

Adding tonic drive woke HS cells from 0 to 6.7 Hz — but direction selectivity stayed at zero. Doubling the visual connectome (2.7M → 5.9M edges) left T4 at 0.0 Hz.

The real reason: T4/T5 direction selectivity comes from neuron-specific timing — Mi9 slow, Mi1/Tm3 fast, Mi4 delayed inhibition. Our model gives every neuron the same τm = 20 ms, τsyn = 5 ms. The connectome gives you wiring, not dynamics. No amount of better input fixes that.
So why is it useful
A QA fuzzer does not need goal-seeking. It needs tireless exploration. Pure randomness cannot get past a title screen; an LLM is too expensive to run for hours. A biological network sits in between — structured, non-deterministic input at zero inference cost, forever.

The lab game has four bugs planted in it. The detector does not know where they are; it only watches for symptoms.

Not reaching the goal is a limit of the model. Spinning in a corner was our bug. Residual bias and saturation in the steering signal kept the fly circling in one place. Retuning adaptation and gain against map coverage — not against goal-seeking — took it from 16 visited cells to 30, from 8.3 spins to 1.9, and from 6 findings per ten runs to 18. Eighteen findings per ten runs, three of the four planted bugs.
